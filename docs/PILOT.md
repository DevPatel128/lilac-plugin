# Run a pilot with three real people

A pilot is a short session where a real person tries Lilac on their own project while you watch. It shows you what confuses people, which you cannot see from your own desk.

**This needs real people. Lilac cannot run a pilot for you.** The simulated visitors and the AI persona reviews that Lilac can produce are useful for catching obvious problems, but they are not people. They do not get tired, lose patience, misread a button or give up. They are not a replacement for a pilot. Do not report them as one.

## 1. Recruit three people

- Pick people who have a real project of their own: a site, an app, or an idea they want to build. Not a made-up exercise.
- Mix them: one beginner, one person who builds regularly, one who is neither.
- Do not pick close friends who will be polite, or people who will say yes to anything.
- Ask in plain words: "Can I watch you try a tool on your own project for 30 minutes? I am testing the tool, not you."
- Tell them up front what you will write down and ask for consent (section 6).
- Run each session alone. Three sessions is enough to see the same problems repeat.

## 2. The 30-minute script

| Minutes | What you do |
|---|---|
| 0-3 | Welcome. Say: "I am testing the tool, not you. If you get stuck, that is my fault, not yours." Ask for consent. Ask them to think out loud. |
| 3-8 | Ask about their project and what they want to get done today. Do not explain Lilac yet. |
| 8-10 | Give them only the install steps from the [README](../README.md), nothing else. Start the clock. |
| 10-25 | Let them try on their own project. Stay quiet. If they are stuck for two minutes, ask "What are you looking for?" before you help. Note every time you do help. |
| 25-28 | Ask: "Would you use this again? Why or why not?" and "What was the most confusing moment?" |
| 28-30 | Thank them. Ask what they agree to share (section 6). |

If something fails, check [TROUBLESHOOTING.md](TROUBLESHOOTING.md) afterwards, not during the session. A failure they hit is a result to record.

## 3. What to watch for

- Long silences, sighs, re-reading the same line.
- Commands they type that you did not expect.
- Words they do not understand.
- Where they look for help and whether they find it.
- Moments they say "oh nice". These matter as much as the problems.

Do not explain, defend or fix while they work. Write it down.

## 4. What to measure

| Measure | How |
|---|---|
| Time to first success | Minutes from "start" to the first output they find useful. Write "never" if they did not get there. |
| Where they got stuck | The step, and what they tried. |
| The questions they asked | Word for word. Every question points to missing guidance. |
| Would they use it again, and why | Yes, maybe or no, plus their own words for the reason. |

## 5. Record it

Fill in one form per person, using the form at the end of this page. Do it within an hour, while you still remember.

## 6. Consent and privacy

- Take notes by hand or in a document. **Record audio, video or the screen only if the person says yes first.** Default is no recording.
- Never write down client data, personal data, passwords, keys or tokens. If a secret appears on screen, do not copy it. Tell the person so they can rotate it.
- Use first names or codes like P1 in notes. Leave out client and company names.
- Share only what they agree to. Ask at the end, and mark the form.
- Delete a person's notes if they ask.

## 7. What to do with the results

1. After all three sessions, list every stuck point and question.
2. Group the ones that mean the same thing.
3. **Fix the top three confusions first.** The ones that more than one person hit come before the ones only one person hit.
4. Change the words or the steps, then run the next three people. Do not claim it is fixed until new people get through it.
5. Keep the forms as your record of what you changed and why.

See [USER-GUIDE.md](USER-GUIDE.md) for the steps the pilot people will follow.
